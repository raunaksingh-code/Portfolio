Workforce & Compensation Analytics — 913K Job Seeker Records

A Business Analytics study applying Exploratory Data Analysis, K-Means clustering and regression modeling to a 913,384-record dataset to explain — and stress-test — salary expectations.

TypeBusiness Analytics Study
Dataset913,384 Records · 18 Variables
TechniquesEDA · Clustering · Regression
StackPython (Pandas, Scikit-learn)
Objective

Apply Business Analytics techniques to a large-scale job-seeker dataset to identify the factors influencing expected salary, segment job seekers into meaningful clusters, and evaluate how well experience, education and skills predict salary expectations — moving from intuition-based to evidence-based workforce strategy.

Methodology & Process

The study was structured around three sequential analytical components, each building on the last.

01

Data Cleaning & Preprocessing

Assessed missing values (833,000+ non-null salary records, 904,000+ non-null experience records), standardized mixed-format salary/experience fields into numeric values, and log-transformed both expected and current salary to correct heavy right-skew before modeling.

02

Exploratory Data Analysis

Examined distributions of salary and experience, ran correlation analysis across numerical variables, and profiled education, specialization, skills and geography against expected salary. Outliers were detected via IQR, z-score and Isolation Forest methods and retained (with transformation) rather than discarded, since they often represent genuine senior-professional profiles.

03

Clustering (K-Means)

Selected experience, education rank, number of skills, and log-transformed current/expected salary as clustering variables, standardized them, and used the Elbow Method to choose K. The elbow curve showed a clear inflection at K = 2, so the final model used two clusters.

04

Regression Modeling

Estimated both a Multiple Linear Regression (experience, education rank, number of skills → expected salary) and a Simple Linear Regression (experience only), deliberately excluding current salary as a predictor to avoid a near-tautological fit.

05

Synthesis Across Methods

Read the clustering and regression results together — the weak cluster separation and the low regression R² were treated as two independent signals pointing to the same underlying conclusion about traditional predictors.

Assumptions & Scope
  • Current salary was deliberately excluded from the regression models despite its near-perfect correlation with expected salary, to avoid tautological rather than explanatory prediction.
  • Both regression models assume linear relationships between predictors and salary — a real limitation the report itself flags, since salary determination may be non-linear in practice.
  • Key explanatory variables — specific job role, industry-specific wage structures, company size, certifications, and individual negotiation behavior — were not available in the dataset and are explicitly named as omitted variables.
  • Expected salary may reflect aspirational rather than market-aligned values, which the report notes as a potential source of noise in the dependent variable itself.
  • Salary and experience variables were log-transformed specifically for clustering to control skew — findings on cluster separation should be read in that transformed space, not raw rupee terms.
Key Findings

Expected salary: median ₹650,000 vs. mean ₹969,451 — a wide gap signalling strong right-skew.

Work experience: median 0.75 years vs. mean 2.39 years — a young, early-career-heavy sample.

The regression model explains only ~5% of the variance in expected salary (Training R² 0.049, Testing R² 0.050).

  • Current salary is the strongest anchor for expected salary (near-perfect correlation); experience shows only a moderate positive correlation; education rank and skill count are weakly correlated.
  • K-Means clustering (K=2, chosen via the Elbow Method) produced only subtle differentiation between clusters — job seekers do not naturally segregate into sharply distinct groups on experience, education, skills or salary alone.
  • In the Multiple Linear Regression, each additional year of experience is associated with ~₹29,200 higher expected salary; the Simple Linear Regression (experience only) puts this at ~₹27,500 — both directionally consistent but explaining little of the total variance.
  • The Mean Absolute Error of the regression exceeds ₹530,000 on both training and testing data — consistent, but large relative to the ₹650,000 median salary itself.
  • The number-of-skills coefficient was negative, suggesting skill relevance and specialization matter more than simply listing more skills.
Key Takeaways
  • Salary expectations among job seekers exhibit significant inequality and high dispersion.
  • Work experience positively influences expected salary, but its explanatory power is limited.
  • Education level and skill count alone are weak predictors of salary expectations.
  • Job seekers do not form strongly differentiated clusters based on traditional attributes.
  • Salary determination is shaped by complex, contextual factors beyond basic demographics — advanced, non-linear models and richer datasets are needed for effective prediction.
